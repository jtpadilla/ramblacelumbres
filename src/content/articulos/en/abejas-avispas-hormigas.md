Bees, wasps and ants, known as hymenopterans, are social insects and an admirable example of organisation. Most species of this order are very useful to people, whether as pollinators or as biological controllers of pests.

## Bees

![Apis mellifera](../../../assets/uploads/2014/07/Apis-mellifica-1.jpg "*Apis mellifera*.")
![Honey bee, abella](../../../assets/uploads/2014/07/Apis-mellifera.jpg "Honey bee, *abella*.")

The honey bee (*abella*) forms large and long-lasting societies. The society is made up of the queen and her daughters, who do all the work: they build the comb from wax, and in its cells the queen lays her eggs. The queen is fertilised only once and remains fertile all her life. She lays up to 2,000 eggs a day over a life of three to five years. The workers decide whether an egg will produce a male, or drone, or a worker. Some individuals act as guards and protect the colony.

When the colony becomes overcrowded, a queen and some workers form a swarm. While the scout bees look for a place to found the new colony, the swarm rests.

## Bumblebees

![Bumblebee, Bombus](../../../assets/uploads/2014/07/Bombylus-maior-copia.jpg "Bumblebee (*Bombus*).")

Bumblebees (*Bombus*) belong to the bee group. They too feed their larvae on pollen and honey, but theirs is a society in which the dominant queen does not hesitate to use physical aggression.

## Wasps

![Polistes nimpha](../../../assets/uploads/2014/07/Avispa-polistes-nimpha-1.jpg "*Polistes nimpha*.")

*Vespes*. Wasp societies consist of the mother and her offspring, all daughters. The males appear when the society is mature, and their only function is to fertilise the females, after which they die. They are fed by the workers.

Wasp societies are temporary and break up in autumn; before that, males and females perform the nuptial flight for fertilisation. The males die and the queens spend the winter dormant, until in spring they start a new nest.

Potter wasps are a subfamily of the common wasps; they are solitary and predatory, and feed by attacking web-spinning spiders and caterpillars. They are so called because they build their nest out of mud, in the shape of a pot or of organ pipes, and attach it to stems or walls. There are two or three generations a year.

## Ants

![Camponotus cruentatus](../../../assets/uploads/2014/07/Camponotus-cruentatus.jpg "*Camponotus cruentatus*.")

*Formigues*. Ants are social species. Size and shape vary from one species to another. Among their characteristics are the jointed antennae and the bump on the slender stalk joining the thorax to the abdomen. Their society is also made up of the mother queen —here there may be several queens— and her daughters, who form very large nests. They are terrestrial animals: they make their nests in the ground and also in tree trunks. Unlike the other hymenopterans, the workers have no wings.

## Antlions

![Adult antlion](../../../assets/uploads/2014/07/Myrmeleontidae.jpg "Adult antlion (family Myrmeleontidae).")

The so-called antlions belong to the order Neuroptera —which means “nerve-winged”— and owe their name to the fact that their prey is mostly ants. The larvae look ferocious, and so is their way of catching their victims: they live buried in sand at the bottom of a funnel, and the small insects that fall in cannot escape and are seized by their jaws.

The adults resemble dragonflies, but lack their power of flight, and are distinguished by their long, sturdy antennae.
